Where the Greeks Fought, 1461 to Today

Every named land and sea battle fought by Greeks for a Greek cause, from the fall of Trebizond to the Turkish invasion of Cyprus in 1974: the revolts under Venetian and Ottoman rule, the War of Independence and the later risings, and every battle of the Greek state, at home and abroad. Where each was fought, against whom, and who won.

How it was made

What counts as a battle

A named land or sea battle: one with its own name in the standard histories or in English or Greek Wikipedia, including named ambushes and sieges with real fighting. Left out: raids on villages or houses, ambushes known only from a description, captures and surrenders without fighting, and phases of a larger battle that would count it twice. Each war’s campaign boxes and battle lists on English and Greek Wikipedia were checked so that nothing named is missed.

Who counts as Greek

Greeks fighting for a Greek cause: the Ρωμιοί who fought as Greeks, the Greek-speaking and Greek-identifying Orthodox communities anywhere (Pontus, Asia Minor, Cyprus, Crete, Macedonia, Thrace, Epirus, the Danubian Principalities in 1821, Theodoro in the Crimea), Albanian-speaking Souliotes, Himariotes and Arvanites and Vlach-speaking Greeks among them. Revolts backed by a foreign power count (the Orlov revolt of 1770, Lambros Katsonis). Greeks serving in foreign armies are left out (the stradioti, Lepanto, Candia, Chesma, Russia’s Greek Legion of 1854, Napoleon’s and Britain’s Greek units, Ottoman Greeks in the Ottoman army), and so are battles with no Greek force (Navarino, 1827). Serbs, Bulgarians and Romanians, also of the Orthodox millet, had their own national movements and are not counted.

Colours and dots

Each battle takes the colour of the Greeks’ opponent: Ottomans and Egyptians (with Ali Pasha, the Barbary corsairs and the Crimean Tatars), Turks (the Turkish Republic and the Turkish Cypriots), Italians and Germans, Bulgarians (the state and the IMRO bands), or others (the British, the Chinese in Korea, the Bolsheviks, the Albanians, the Great Powers). Civil-war battles are Greek v Greek, blue. Each place is drawn as a small pie: its area grows with the number of battles there, and the slices give the number against each opponent, starting at 12 o’clock and running clockwise in the order of the legend.

Wins, draws and losses

The victor is the side that won on the battlefield: it held the field, routed the enemy, took the position or destroyed the fleet. Strategic outcomes and disagreements between Greek and Turkish (or other) accounts are in the notes. For a Greek unit inside a larger Allied battle, the result is the battle’s. A draw is an indecisive battle, or one that both sides claimed with no clear verdict. The 55 civil-war battles are listed and mapped but not scored, and so is the one battle with an unknown result.

Research

Fourteen research batches by period, each following the same written rules; then a check of the entries against Greek and English Wikipedia, which added battles from their categories and the Civil War of 1946–49; then a focused fact-check of every uncertain entry, which settled results and dates and dropped 121 entries, listed under Left out. Positions: 231 known, 238 approximate, 39 only within their region.
